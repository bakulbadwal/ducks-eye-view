Eye Exama hands-on lab

How a robot's camera gets calibrated

Microduck goes to the optician. A checkerboard is the eye chart, every photo of it held at a new angle is one view, the lens dial is the distortion model, and the prescription card is the camera matrix K. You'll measure the numbers Duck's-Eye View took as given, with Zhang's method, the one inside OpenCV's calibrateCamera, running for real in your browser. About 70 minutes of play.

0 · The exam room: what the prescription card holds

A camera turns a direction into a pixel. In Duck's-Eye View's map room you dragged a duck around and watched the pinhole put it in the frame; there the camera's numbers were given. Here they're the unknowns. The intrinsics are five numbers in a 3 × 3 matrix K, plus two for the lens. Calibration finds them from photos of something whose shape you know exactly. Every term with a wavy red underline is tappable.

Say it out loud
Intrinsics turn a direction into a pixel: focal lengths fx and fy in pixels, the principal point (cx, cy) where the optical axis lands, a skew, and the lens's own bend, k1 and k2. Calibration measures them from photos of a pattern whose shape you know.

1 · One photo: the tracing card

The chart is flat, so put it at Z = 0. Then a pinhole maps every chart point (X, Y) to its pixel through one 3 × 3 matrix: s·[u, v, 1]ᵀ = H·[X, Y, 1]ᵀ, with H = K [r1 r2 t] (Zhang's eq. 2). That's a homography: 9 numbers, but only 8 matter because its scale is free. Each corner gives two equations, so the 54 corners give a 108 × 9 matrix L with L·h = 0, and the best h is the singular vector of L's smallest singular value. That's the direct linear transform. Steps 1 to 3 photograph the chart with the lens dial at 0, so a homography is exact; step 4 puts the lens back.

Covers Zhang App. A: the 2n × 9 system L x = 0, "the right singular vector of L associated with the smallest singular value", and "L poorly conditioned numerically … much better results … by performing a simple data normalization" (citing Hartley, 1995)

Where is the chart's zero?

The DLT minimises an algebraic quantity, |L h|, not a distance in pixels, and what that quantity weighs depends on your coordinates. Number the corners from a point 1 m off the chart (say, the corner of the shop wall), or photograph the chart from across the room, and the un-normalised solve gets lopsided. Hartley's fix: shift each set of points so its centroid is at zero, scale it so the mean distance is √2, solve, and undo the shift. One hundred photos per run, each with fresh noise.

Say it out loud
One photo of a flat chart is one homography, H = K[r1 r2 t]: stack two equations per corner and take the smallest singular vector. Normalise both point sets first, or the answer depends on where you put the origin and how far away the chart is.

2 · Two clues per photo: why one view isn't enough

H = λK[r1 r2 t], and r1, r2 are two columns of a rotation: perpendicular, and the same length. Undo K and those two facts become two equations, h1ᵀBh2 = 0 and h1ᵀBh1 = h2ᵀBh2, on one matrix, B = K⁻ᵀK⁻¹. B is symmetric, so it's six numbers b, and since its scale is free that's five unknowns: fx, fy, cx, cy and the skew. Every photo adds two rows to a matrix V with V·b = 0 (Zhang's eq. 9). Count: one photo, 2 equations; two photos, 4; three photos, 6 ≥ 5. The pose of each photo (3 numbers of rotation, 3 of position) eats the other 6 of H's 8.

Covers Zhang §2.3: "we can only obtain 2 constraints on the intrinsic parameters" · §3.1: n ≥ 3 gives a unique b; n = 2 needs the skew-free assumption · §4, Proposition 1: a plane parallel to an earlier one adds no constraints

Guess first!
Five square-on photos, each at a different place, distance and spin. How many zero singular values does V have?
4. Parallel planes meet the plane at infinity in the same line, so every square-on photo repeats the same two equations (Zhang's Proposition 1). Spinning the chart in its own plane doesn't help, and nor does sliding a tilted chart around without changing its tilt: that's a pure translation, and Zhang rules it out too.

How flat is too flat?

Exactly parallel is exactly degenerate. Nearly parallel is nearly degenerate, and noise finishes the job. Each run: 200 sessions of 3 photos at 500 mm, tilted by the same angle about random axes, with 0.5 px of corner noise. A session fails if B comes out impossible (not positive definite, so no real camera has it) or fx is more than 50% off.

Guess first!
Three photos tilted 5°, σ = 0.5 px. Of 200 sessions, how many blow up?
45 of 200, and the ones that survive are 13% off in fx on average. At 2° it's 150 of 200; from 20° on, 3 to 5. Zhang's own simulation (his camera, his chart) lost 40% of trials at 5° and did best around 45°.
Say it out loud
Each photo of a plane gives two equations on B = K⁻ᵀK⁻¹, which has five unknowns: three photos in general, two if you assume no skew. Photos of parallel planes repeat the same two equations, so what counts is how differently the chart is tilted, not how many photos you take.

3 · The closed form: writing the prescription

With three or more photos, stack every photo's two rows into V and take the right singular vector of its smallest singular value: that's b, so that's B. Zhang's Appendix B reads K straight out of B: cy = (B12B13 − B11B23)/(B11B22 − B12²), then λ, fx = √(λ/B11), fy, the skew and cx. Then every photo's pose follows from K⁻¹H: r1 = λK⁻¹h1, r2 = λK⁻¹h2, r3 = r1 × r2, t = λK⁻¹h3, and the nearest true rotation (App. C). No iterations, no starting guess: a closed form.

Say it out loud
Stack two rows per photo into V, take the singular vector of the smallest singular value, and read K straight out of B; then each photo's rotation and position follow from K⁻¹H. It's exact without noise and fast with it, and it's a starting point, not the answer.

4 · The lens dial: radial distortion

Now the real lens goes back in. A wide, cheap lens bends straight lines, more the further they are from the principal point. Zhang's model moves each point along its ray from the principal point: in normalised coordinates, x̆ = x(1 + k1·r² + k2·r⁴), with r² = x² + y². Negative k1 is barrel distortion, pulling the edges in; positive is pincushion. Microduck's lens has k1 = −0.347 and k2 = +0.146. A homography can't bend, so every step so far is now wrong, as you'll see.

Covers Zhang §3.3: the first two radial terms; "the distortion function is totally dominated by the radial components, and especially dominated by the first term"; distortion by linear least squares, k = (DᵀD)⁻¹Dᵀd (eq. 13)

The linear fix, starting from the lens-blind closed form: what sign does it give k1?

Only refining everything together untangles them.
Say it out loud
A real lens moves each point along its ray from the principal point by 1 + k1·r² + k2·r⁴; negative k1 is barrel. The closed form can't see that, so its K and even a linear k1 come out wrong, and only the joint refinement untangles them.

5 · Better one, or two? Refinement and its score

The optician's last step: flip between two lenses, keep the sharper one, repeat in smaller steps. The math version is Levenberg–Marquardt: start from the closed form and the linear k1, k2, and move all the numbers at once (fx, fy, cx, cy, k1, k2, and every photo's 3 rotation and 3 position numbers: 6 + 6 × 12 = 78 here, with the skew held at 0) to shrink the total squared distance between each corner as seen and where the card predicts it. The Jacobian says which way is downhill; λ blends a Gauss–Newton jump with a cautious gradient step. The score at the end is the reprojection RMS, the one number OpenCV's calibrateCamera returns.

Covers Zhang §3.2–3.3: maximum-likelihood refinement of eq. 14 with Levenberg–Marquardt, rotations as Rodrigues vectors · OpenCV calibrateCamera: "Run the global Levenberg-Marquardt optimization algorithm to minimize the reprojection error"; returns "the overall RMS re-projection error"

The corners were found with σ = 0.25 px of noise on each axis. After refinement, what's the best RMS you could hope for?
The noise itself: σ√2 = 0.354 px per corner (two axes). The exam ends at 0.350 px, a hair under, as it should be: fitting 78 free numbers to 1,296 corner coordinates soaks up some of the noise (on average the floor drops to about σ√2·√(1 − 78/1296) ≈ 0.343 px). An RMS far below the corner detector's noise means the model is fitting noise, not the lens.

A calibration with the lens dial held at 0 scores RMS 4.36 px. Drop the 6 photos with the worst error and solve again: the RMS halves to 2.08 px. Is it a better calibration?

Say it out loud
Refinement minimises the total reprojection error over every corner in every photo with Levenberg–Marquardt, starting from the closed form. The RMS it reports is how well the model fits these photos, not how right it is: judge it on photos it never saw, and suspect any fix that only lowers the score.

6 · The fitting: a good session, and what a bad one costs

You control four things in a real session: how many photos, how much the chart is tilted, whether its corners reach every part of the frame, and how sharply the corners are found (sub-pixel refinement, like OpenCV's cornerSubPix, places them to a fraction of a pixel; σ here is per axis). What it costs the duck

Microduck's detector works on the frame turned a quarter turn clockwise, 720 × 1280, upright: upright pixel (x′, y′) comes from stream pixel (y′, 719 − x′). Its bearing is b = (box centre x′ / 720) · 2 − 1, from −1 (hard left) to +1 (hard right). So left–right for the duck runs along the stream's vertical axis: fy and cy set the angle, and the principal point sits at x′ = 719 − 474.52 = 244.5, 115 px left of the upright frame's middle. Say it out loud

What's exact here, and what's a teaching model

  • The real math, written out in js/core.js: the direct linear transform for a homography and Hartley's normalisation (Zhang App. A); the two constraints per homography, V b = 0 by singular value decomposition, and K from B (§3.1, App. B); the extrinsics from K⁻¹H and the nearest rotation (App. C); radial distortion by linear least squares (eq. 13); Levenberg–Marquardt on the total reprojection error (eq. 14) with Rodrigues rotations; the reprojection RMS as OpenCV's calibrateCamera computes it, √(Σ|e|²/N). The SVD (one-sided Jacobi), the symmetric eigen-decomposition (Jacobi), the 3 × 3 inverse and the Cholesky solve are hand-written; tests/check.js checks them, and that DLT and Zhang recover a known H and K exactly with no noise.
  • Teaching models, labelled on the page: the photos are synthetic. The chart is a perfect 9 × 6 grid at 25 mm; each photo's pose is drawn from a seed and kept fully in frame; the corner detector is the true corner plus Gaussian noise of σ pixels per axis (real detectors are worse on steep, blurry or dim photos, which this ignores: Zhang notes foreshortening "is not considered" in his simulation either). The true camera is Microduck's published calibration, rounded, with k1 and k2 only; its tangential terms and k3 are left out. LM holds the skew at 0 by default, as OpenCV does; OpenCV's own first guess is simpler than Zhang's (principal point at the frame's middle, focal lengths by least squares), and its current 4.x source solves the same least-squares problem with a sparse bundle-adjustment formulation by default; this lab follows Zhang's paper. Its Jacobian is numeric (central differences), not analytic.
  • The head turn is geometry, not the robot's code. The bearing formula and the quarter turn are Microduck's (duck-detect, uyvy); turning a bearing into an angle with a calibration, and assuming the camera looks straight down the head's axis, is ours. Mounting the camera at an angle would add an extrinsic offset this lab doesn't model.
  • The directions are real; the numbers are this toy's. A real session has blur, lighting, a chart that isn't quite flat, and a detector that sometimes mislabels a corner. The failure modes here (flat photos, an uncovered frame, a model without a lens) are the real ones.
